Where We Started

Our team started with a base network from a previous homework assignment. It had convolutional layers in it, so on paper it was a convolutional network:

Conv2D(10 filters, 5x5) -> Conv2D(10 filters, 5x5) -> MaxPool
-> Flatten -> Dense(128, L1) -> Dense(64, softplus, L1) -> Dense(10)

It got 98.23% validation accuracy. That is not a bad number, but we could not tune our way past it, and we spent a couple of runs trying before we looked at the model more carefully.

What changed our approach was counting where the parameters actually were.

Parameter split in the inherited model
ComponentParametersShare of model
Convolutional layers~2,8002%
Dense classifier head~137,00098%

Our beginning model was a CNN with a large dense head and a small convolutional feature extractor. With 98% of the parameters sitting in the classification head, we wanted the convolutional layers to learn richer features of the digits and then pass those features to a classification head with fewer hidden layers.

Once we saw that, the plan for the rest of the project was obvious: increase capacity in the feature extractor. This way, the convolutional layers would learn features of the digit, then apply a shallower decision layer on top of already-good features.

How We Ran The Search

We did not grid search. We had eight or so hyperparameters we cared about, and even three values each would have meant thousands of training runs. Instead we changed one thing at a time, trained, logged the result, kept whatever won, and moved on to the next thing. We ordered the stages by how much we expected each one to matter, so the expensive compute went where it was most likely to pay off.

Our approach loosely followed two rules:

The first was one change per run. If a run changes two things and the accuracy moves, you have learned nothing about either one. We broke this rule twice early on and it cost us, which we get into below.

The second was setting a noise floor before we started reading results. Our validation set was 12,000 images. The standard error on an accuracy estimate near 99% works out to roughly 0.09%, so any difference smaller than about 0.2 percentage points is around 24 images and is not distinguishable from randomness. Several of our runs moved the number by less than that. We logged those as ties rather than improvements. This probably saved us an afternoon of tuning toward noise.

Taking Out The Choices We Could Not Justify

The inherited model had an L1 regularizer on both dense layers and a softplus activation on the second one. Both of these were randomly applied, and did not have a reason for the addition.

L1 regularization pushes weights to exactly zero. It is a sparsity tool, which is why it shows up in LASSO, and sparsity was not what we wanted. We wanted smooth generalization, and the tools for that are dropout and batch normalization. The L1 penalties were working against the network rather than for it. Softplus is a smoothed version of ReLU with no real advantage on this problem and slightly worse gradient behavior, so that came out too.

Removing both of those, trimming the dense head down to one hidden layer, and right-sizing the convolutions took us from 98.23% to 98.75% over three runs.

This is where we broke our rules—runs 2 through 4 each changed more than one thing, so we cannot cleanly say how much of that half point came from any single edit. We are reporting the phase as a whole rather than claiming attribution we cannot support.

Adding Depth

The biggest single improvement in the whole project came from adding a second convolutional block.

Conv(32) -> Conv(32) -> MaxPool   # 28x28 -> 14x14
Conv(64) -> Conv(64) -> MaxPool   # 14x14 -> 7x7

That took us from 98.75% to 99.04%. Nothing else we tried moved the number as much.

One convolutional block can only learn edges and strokes. A second block gets to compose those strokes into larger parts, like loops, crossings, and the closed upper region that is the difference between an 8 and a 3. With only one block, the dense layer has to assemble all of those relationships itself, and a fully connected layer is not good at that.

We also moved from 5x5 kernels down to 3x3. Two stacked 3x3 convolutions cover the same receptive field as one 5x5, use fewer parameters, and get an extra nonlinearity in between. More small filters beat fewer large ones, which was also what our instructor suggested.

The third change in this phase was padding. Without padding, every 3x3 convolution takes two pixels off each dimension, so by the time we had four convolutions and two pooling layers we were down to 4x4x32, which is 512 features reaching the classifier. Adding same padding means only the pooling reduces size, so the image goes 28 to 14 to 7 and the flatten layer gets 7x7x64, or 3,136 features. That is roughly six times more information surviving to the dense layer, and it also made the arithmetic predictable enough that we could work out the flatten size in our heads instead of running summary every time.

After that we doubled the filters in block 2 from 32 to 64. That moved us from 99.04% to 99.09%, which is inside our noise floor. We took that as a signal that capacity was no longer the thing holding us back.

The Problem Changed

Looking at the log, the accuracy column alone was not telling us much anymore. The gap between training and validation was.

Training accuracy had been sitting near 99.8% since the third run. The model was not having any trouble learning the training set. It had done that several runs earlier. At this point, we had solved the capacity problem, clearly showing that our model could learn the training set. What our next refinements would target would be generalization—closing the performance gap between the training and validation sets.

Regularization

Batch normalization on its own made things slightly worse. Adding it to each convolutional block moved validation accuracy from 99.09% down to 98.91%. Because the drop was small, we kept that result in the log rather than dropping it. What we eventually concluded is that batch normalization was doing something useful, but conditionally. It stabilized training enough that we could use heavier dropout later without the model failing to converge. On its own it did not help.

Dropout in the convolutional blocks was where we found significant gains in closing the gap. Twenty-five percent after each pooling layer got us to 99.22%. Pushing it to 30% held validation equal while pulling training accuracy down from 99.71% to 99.42%, which was the gap closing.

Dropout in the dense head was the piece we had been missing. Our final model had 468,202 parameters, and 403,000 of them, about 86%, were in the single connection between the flatten layer and the Dense(128). That is where memorization happens, so that is where the heaviest dropout belongs. We added 40% dropout after the dense layer, raised the epochs to 50, and got 99.44%.

Lessons Learned

The first time we added dropout we left the epoch count at 10, and dropout looked like it made the model worse. It had not. Dropout randomly switches off units during training, so each pass through the data teaches the model less and it needs more passes to get to the same place. Ten epochs did not allow for enough passes through to train the model. Raising the epochs to 50 showed the improvement that had been there the whole time. We saw that most models seemed to level out somewhere between 30-40 epochs.

The lesson we took from that is that regularization strength and training length are tied together. Changing one without the other gives you a misleading answer, and we came close to writing off a good idea because of it.

The second problem was subtler. Calling fit without rebuilding the model first continues training from the weights that are already there instead of starting over. One of our runs reported 50 epochs but was really running epochs 51 through 100. We caught it because epoch 1 came back with 99.86% training accuracy, which is impossible for a network that has not been trained. A fresh model should start somewhere around 85 to 90%. After that we rebuilt and recompiled the model before every single fit.

Final Training

Once the architecture was locked in, the validation split had done its job. Holding back 12,000 images to answer a question we had already answered just meant training on less data than we had, so we retrained the final configuration on all 60,000 training images.

The one judgment call was the epoch count, because with no validation set there is nothing for early stopping to watch. Validation loss during the architecture search had flattened out around epoch 37 and had not diverged by epoch 50, so we used 40.

Conv2D(32, 3x3, same) -> Conv2D(32, 3x3, same)
  -> BatchNorm -> MaxPool -> Dropout(0.30)
Conv2D(64, 3x3, same) -> Conv2D(64, 3x3, same)
  -> BatchNorm -> MaxPool -> Dropout(0.30)
Flatten -> Dense(128, ReLU) -> Dropout(0.40) -> Dense(10, softmax)

Test accuracy: 99.52%. That is 48 misclassified images out of 10,000.

What We Would Do Differently

The two diagnostics we ran, counting where the parameters were and watching the train/validation gap, were both free and both changed what we did next. We ran the first one four runs in and the second one six runs in. If we had run them at the start we could have gotten to the same model in about half the runs.

However, our negative results turned out to be useful ones. Batch normalization looked like a failure by itself and turned out to be a prerequisite for the thing that actually worked. Dropout looked like a failure because we had the epoch count wrong. Neither of those shows up in a log that only records the runs that improved the number, and we would have drawn the wrong conclusions from a cleaner looking log.

Error Analysis

Group Project 1 — MNIST Image Classification

Well Classified And Misclassified Examples

Our final model classified 9,952 of the 10,000 test images correctly and got 48 wrong. Because 48 is a small enough number to inspect individually, we looked at every one of them rather than sampling.

We sorted both sets by the model's own confidence rather than showing a random selection. Sorting this way separates two different kinds of behavior. The most confident correct predictions show us what the model considers an easy case. The most confident errors show us where the model was certain and still wrong, which is a much more interesting failure than a case where it was simply unsure.

What The Model Finds Easy

Every image in the confidently correct grid came back at a confidence of 1.000, which means the softmax assigned essentially all of its probability to a single class. These digits share a set of properties:

They are centered in the frame with consistent margins. Stroke width is even and thick enough to survive downsampling. The loops that define a digit are unambiguously open or closed, so there is no question whether a 9 has a sealed head or whether a 6 has a closed bottom. The overall shape matches the canonical form of the digit rather than a personal variation. None of them are unusually slanted.

One thing we noticed in the confidently correct set is that 4s are characteristically absent. The eighteen most confident correct predictions include multiple 9s, 3s and 2s but not a single 4. This is consistent with what the confusion matrix shows later: 4 and 9 are the pair the model is least sure about, so even the 4s it gets right are classified with a little less certainty than other digits.

What The Model Gets Wrong

The misclassified images look nothing like the correct ones. They fall into three groups.

Broken or incomplete strokes. Several of these digits are drawn so thinly or so faintly that after downsampling the stroke is fragmented into disconnected pixels. One image labeled as a 7 is barely more than a diagonal smear with a gap in the middle, and the model called it a 1. The defining feature of the digit was not preserved in the image.

Ambiguous loop closure. A number of the errors hinge on whether a single loop is open or closed. A 4 with its top corner touching becomes a 9. A 9 whose upper bowl does not quite meet becomes a 4. A 6 whose tail crosses back over itself becomes an 8. In each case the difference between the two digits is a few pixels in one location.

Unusual handwriting. The remaining errors are digits written in ways that are technically legible but far from the canonical form. One 3 in the set is so thick and rounded that both of its curves close into loops, which is the definition of an 8. One 5 is written as a single continuous S shape with no horizontal bar at the top.

The bottom line is that a meaningful portion of these 48 images are ones a person would also hesitate over. We discuss that further in the section on whether 100 percent accuracy is achievable.

Two of these are not independent errors but two halves of the same underlying confusion.

The 3 And 5 Cluster

The model called a 3 a 5 three times and called a 5 a 3 four times, for seven errors between them. This is the single largest source of error in the model and it runs in both directions, which tells us the two digits are genuinely entangled rather than one simply being harder than the other.

Structurally, a 3 and a 5 share almost everything. Both have a horizontal component at the top, both have a large curve opening to the left at the bottom, and both occupy the same region of the frame. The difference is confined to the upper left: a 5 has a vertical stroke descending into a horizontal bar, while a 3 has an outward curve. When a 5 is written quickly the top bar often curves, and at that point the two digits differ by a handful of pixels in one corner.

The 4 And 9 Cluster

The model called a 9 a 4 five times and called a 4 a 9 twice, for seven errors. Unlike the 3 and 5 case, this one is asymmetric. The model is more than twice as likely to read a 9 as a 4 than the reverse.

The distinguishing feature here is a single property: whether the upper loop is closed. A 9 has a sealed head sitting on a vertical stroke. A 4 has an open angular junction in the same position with a descender below it. If a 9 is written with a small gap at the top of its head, the remaining shape is a 4. The asymmetry makes sense given how people write: leaving a loop slightly open is a common result of writing fast, while accidentally sealing the open corner of a 4 requires overshooting the stroke, which happens less often.

Why The Model Loses This Information

Both clusters depend on a small, local detail rather than on overall shape, and that is exactly the kind of feature a convolutional network is structured to discard.

Our architecture applies two rounds of 2x2 max pooling, taking the representation from 28 by 28 down to 14 by 14 and then to 7 by 7. Max pooling deliberately gives up spatial precision in exchange for translation invariance, which is what makes the model robust to a digit being written slightly off center. The cost is that by the seventh row and column of the final feature map, each cell summarizes a 4 by 4 region of the original image. A two-pixel gap at the top of a 9 sits entirely inside one of those cells and may not survive the pooling operation. The model is built to stop looking that closely, and that same property is what gives it 99.5 percent accuracy on everything else.

The hardest digit to recognize is 6, at a 0.94 percent error rate, followed closely by 9 at 0.89 percent. The 6 errors are unusual in that they scatter across five different wrong answers rather than concentrating on one, which suggests the problem is not a single confusable neighbor but that 6 is written in a wider variety of ways than other digits.

The easiest digit is 8, with a single error out of 974 images, a rate of 0.10 percent. This surprised us, because 8 has the most complex shape of any digit and we expected it to be among the hardest. The complexity appears to work in its favor. Two stacked closed loops is a distinctive signature that nothing else in the digit set resembles, so once the model sees it there is little to confuse it with.

The 8 Is A Sink For Uncertainty

The last column of the table above reveals something interesting that is not obvious when looking at only the error rate. While 8 is almost never misread as something else, it is the answer the model gives most often when it is wrong. Nine of the 48 errors were images of other digits predicted as 8, more than for any other class.

Digits 3, 4, 6, 7 and 9 were all misread as 8 at least once. The common factor is that when a digit is written thickly enough for its strokes to merge, or when an open curve closes accidentally, the resulting blob has the loop structure that defines an 8. The model appears to treat closed loops as strong evidence for 8, which is correct almost all of the time and produces these errors in the small number of cases where the loops are artifacts of thick handwriting rather than intentional.

The mirror image of this is the digit 7, which was the predicted answer for only one error in the entire test set. Whatever features the model uses to identify a 7 are ones that other digits rarely trigger by accident.

Is 100 Percent Accuracy Possible?

Our answer is no—based on the dataset and the variance in handwriting, 100% accuracy is not possible.

The Limit Is In The Data

Every dataset has some level of error that no model can go below, because some of the inputs do not contain enough information to determine the answer. For MNIST this shows up in three forms.

Some images are genuinely ambiguous. Looking through our 48 errors, a number of them are digits that two people would label differently. If the image does not contain enough information for a human to decide, there is nothing for the model to learn that would fix it.

Some labels are wrong. MNIST was assembled by having people write digits and recording what they were asked to write. Where a writer produced a malformed character, the label records the intent rather than what actually appeared on the page. A model that learns the true shape of a 4 will disagree with the label on an image where someone drew something closer to a 9, and it will be penalized for being right.

The format itself discards information. Each image is a 28 by 28 grayscale grid. A person writing the original digit had stroke order, pressure, pen lift and full resolution available. All of that is gone by the time the image reaches the model. Several of our errors involve strokes so thin that downsampling broke them into disconnected fragments, and the information needed to reconnect them does not exist anywhere in the file.

What The Evidence In Our Own Results Shows

The most direct evidence sits in our own confident error grid. Those images were misclassified with confidence at or near 1.00, meaning the model was not hesitating. It looked at the image, found strong evidence for the wrong answer, and committed.

This matters because there are two different reasons a model can be wrong. If it is wrong while uncertain, the signal was there and the model failed to weigh it correctly, which is a problem more training or a better architecture could fix. If it is wrong while certain, the image genuinely looks like the wrong answer, and the fix would have to be a model that disagrees with the visual evidence in front of it. Our errors are overwhelmingly of the second kind.

Where That Leaves Us

Our 99.52 percent leaves 48 errors. We would estimate that a stronger approach, using augmentation and an ensemble, could recover perhaps half of them, since those techniques mainly help with unusual but legible handwriting. The remaining images are ones where the label and the picture disagree, and no amount of modeling resolves that.

A more useful goal than 100 percent accuracy would be a model that is well calibrated: one that is confident when it is right and appropriately uncertain when the image is ambiguous. By that standard our model has room to improve, because the errors it makes are ones it makes confidently. A model that had flagged those 48 images as uncertain would be more useful in practice than one that got a few more of them right while remaining equally overconfident about the rest.
